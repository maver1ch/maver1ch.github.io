## Problem

Tutoring teachers in Vietnam grade handwritten math by hand.

- **Slow** — 2–3 hours to grade one class.
- **Costly** — a paid assistant charges about 5,000 VND per paper.
- **Hard to see at first** — it took two hypotheses and 71 teacher interviews to find this pain.

<PhaseMarker label="Phase 1 — Discovery: two hypotheses killed in two months" />

We ran 71 teacher interviews, in person and online. I ran the first 20–30 myself; later the marketing team ran them and I turned what they heard into product.

<DecisionBlock
  decision="Kill hypothesis 1, the AI tutor for students, in week 4."
  why="I shipped the tutor MVP in 6 hours. But not every student has a device, most just want homework done fast, and in EdTech students are not the ones who pay. Teachers pay, and we had a large pool of them to interview."
  tradeoff="We dropped a working MVP after four weeks."
/>

<DecisionBlock
  decision="Hypothesis 2: a grading tool plus a progress dashboard for teachers. Cut the dashboard."
  why="We shipped a dashboard demo that tracked each student's progress and learning path. Teachers praised it, but when we invited them to use it, they did not. Their energy rose only when the talk turned to their own time: they wanted to teach more students, not read more charts."
  tradeoff="Learning paths and weak-spot analytics left the roadmap."
/>

<PhaseMarker label="Phase 2 — Product: AI grading, teacher in control" highlight />

  tradeoff="Teachers still collect papers and upload them by hand."

The teacher creates an exam and uploads the class's papers. Grading runs in **background batches**, aiming at 20–30 minutes per class, and an **email** says when papers are ready. Nothing leaves until the teacher **approves**. Students sending papers on Zalo OA, and reports going straight to parents, were planned next (dashed).
</ArchitectureSection>

<ArchitectureSection
  step="02"
  title="AI Grading Pipeline: Rubric Once, Grade Many"
  image="/assets/case-studies/wizzdom/02-grading-pipeline.png"
  alt="Every AI step on Gemini 2.5 Pro: exam split into questions with a rubric of steps per question; each paper is OCR'd by Mathpix, Gemini Vision finds the answer blocks, a weak-spot note shows messy handwriting fooling both, a fuzzy match ties each one to its question, Gemini reads the image and the LaTeX and grades step by step with an error type, line mapping pins notes to the handwriting, and the teacher reviews with a flag warning, can re-grade one question, and approves"
  highlight
  challenge="Handwritten math, loose question labels, many valid methods, and credit for each step."
>
**Gemini** splits the exam and writes a **rubric of steps** per question. On each paper, **Mathpix** OCRs the math, Gemini Vision finds each answer, and a **fuzzy match** ties labels like "1a" to "Câu 1.a". Gemini grades step by step, names the **error type** and accepts other valid methods. **Line mapping** pins every note to the student's line.
</ArchitectureSection>

<ArchitectureSection
  step="03"
  title="Teacher Review: Errors on the Handwriting"
  image="/assets/case-studies/wizzdom/teacher-review.jpg"
  alt="Grading screen: rubric steps marked right or wrong on the left, the student's handwritten paper with an AI error note pinned to the wrong line on the right, and a re-grade button per question"

  challenge="The teacher has to check AI grades fast, or the time saved is lost."
>
Each step is marked **right or wrong** next to the paper, and AI notes sit **on the exact line** the student wrote. Teachers can edit, draw, or **re-grade one question**, then approve. All of it came from **teacher demos and interviews** during the build: check faster, trust more, and mark the way they already do on paper.
</ArchitectureSection>

<ArchitectureSection
  step="04"
  title="Report for Parents, Editable by the Teacher"
  image="/assets/case-studies/wizzdom/student-report.jpg"

Next to the grades, the teacher gets a **report written to the parent**: the score, which questions were done, right or missing, then **strengths, weaknesses and how to improve**, each tied to a question. Teachers **edit any line** in place and **export a PDF** to send. Sending it straight to parents on Zalo was my next idea.
</ArchitectureSection>

<ArchitectureSection
  step="05"
  title="System Architecture on AWS"
  image="/assets/case-studies/wizzdom/03-aws-architecture.png"
  alt="Teacher web app on Vercel calls API Gateway and an API Lambda; uploads land in S3, EventBridge routes exam and paper uploads through SQS to a worker Lambda that calls Mathpix and Gemini; RDS PostgreSQL stores the data and SES emails the teacher; Zalo OA is drawn dashed as planned"

**React + TypeScript** on Vercel with Google sign-in. **API Gateway → Lambda** (Python) serves the web app. Every upload to **S3** is an event: **EventBridge** sends an exam to preparation and a paper to grading, through **SQS** to a worker Lambda that calls Mathpix and Gemini. **RDS PostgreSQL** keeps exams, rubrics and teacher edits; **SES** emails the teacher.
</ArchitectureSection>

<PhaseMarker label="Phase 3 — Pilot: measure, find the root cause, fix" highlight />

<DecisionBlock
  decision="Flag messy handwriting once per student, not answer by answer."
  why="v1 on Gemini 2.5 Pro graded 2x faster than by hand, but agreed with teacher grades only 60–70% of the time. I traced the misses: the model solved the math but misread messy handwriting. Handwriting does not change, so the teacher flags each student once. Neat writers reached ~95% agreement; flagged papers get a close check."
  tradeoff="Teachers still check flagged students closely."
/>

After I moved to an advisory role, the team switched to Gemini 3 Pro, whose stronger vision lifted agreement above 80%.

## Outcome

- **Paid pilot** — 2–3 tutoring classes, **1,000+ papers**, teachers paid for the grading service.
- **2x faster** than grading by hand; **60–70%** agreement in v1, **~95%** for students with neat handwriting.
- **Later, by the team:** **80%+** with Gemini 3 Pro.
- **Teacher in control** — nothing reaches a parent before the teacher approves.
- **Discovery before code** — 71 teacher interviews, two hypotheses killed in two months.
